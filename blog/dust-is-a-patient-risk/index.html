In a hospital, dust is a patient risk.

Cut into a ceiling in an office building and you have made a mess. Do the same thing in a hospital and you have released airborne particles into a building full of people whose immune systems cannot handle them. That single difference drives more of our healthcare design work than any code in the stack.

The clinical problem

Construction dust in a hospital is not an aesthetic issue. Disturbing old ceiling cavities, wall assemblies and above ceiling spaces releases particulate, and some of it carries fungal spores that are harmless to the crew doing the work and dangerous to an immunocompromised patient two rooms away.

The exposure path is the air handling system, which is very good at moving whatever is in one space into several others. That is why the question is never just how do we do this work. It is how do we do this work without changing what the patients are breathing.

What an ICRA is

An Infection Control Risk Assessment is the document that answers that question before the work starts. It evaluates the type of construction activity against the risk level of the areas around it, and the result sets the requirements: what barriers go up, whether the work area is held at negative pressure, how the crew enters and exits, how debris leaves the building, and how the space is cleaned before it is turned back over.

The construction activity types run from minor inspection work at one end to major demolition at the other, and together with the risk level of the surrounding area they set a precaution class that escalates accordingly. Class I precautions might mean nothing more than careful work and immediate cleanup. Class IV precautions in a high risk area mean sealed barriers, an anteroom, controlled pressure and a documented cleaning protocol.

Why it belongs in the design, not the field

The common failure is treating infection control as the contractor's responsibility to figure out on arrival. By then the sequence is set, the barrier locations are whatever the walls allow, and the hospital finds out about the disruption at the same time as everyone else.

On the chilled water replacement at the Asheville VA, we produced the ICRA plans and drawings as part of the design package. That meant the barriers, containment and protections for patients, staff and sensitive equipment were drawn and bid along with the piping, and the phasing plan was built around them rather than the other way around.

The same requirement shaped the work at Brockton, where an inpatient mental health building went through four phases with only one or two wards offline at a time, and at Bedford, where site investigations ran through 27 occupied buildings. In both cases infection control procedures and coordination with hospital departments were part of the scope from the beginning.

What it costs and what it buys

Containment costs money and it costs schedule. Barriers get built and then demolished, work areas get smaller, and phases multiply. Owners sometimes look at that line and ask whether it can be trimmed.

What it buys is the ability to do the work at all. Without a containment plan the only alternative on a sensitive floor is to close it, and closing a floor costs far more than sealing one. The barrier is the reason the hospital keeps operating around your project.

The takeaway

If the design for your renovation does not include an infection control plan, the project is not fully designed yet. Draw the barriers with the ductwork. It is the difference between a job that moves through an occupied building and one that stops it.
