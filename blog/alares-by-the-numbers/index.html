Alares by the numbers.

We do not spend much time talking about scale. The work is mostly technical and the interesting part is usually a single detail, a footing that had to clear a tunnel, a control sequence fighting itself. But every so often it is worth adding it up. Here is what nineteen years looks like in numbers, and what each one actually meant on site.

19 years

Alares was founded in 2007 by Don Maggioli, a West Point graduate and former Green Beret officer. Nearly everything since has been federal and healthcare work: VA medical centers, national cemeteries, GSA buildings. That narrowness is the point. The federal standards, the VA handbooks, the infection control requirements and the phasing constraints of a hospital that never closes are all knowledge you only get by doing it repeatedly.

11 hospital campuses

Across 11 VA hospital campuses in New England, we retrocommissioned existing building systems and identified opportunities for projected energy savings of 15 to 20 percent. The recommendations focused on operational improvements to existing equipment rather than a capital replacement program.

For a facility manager, those findings provide a starting point for evaluating operational improvements before committing to equipment replacement. Identified savings are projections, not a record of measured reductions after implementation.

45 buildings, 11 campuses

Across California, our structural team evaluated 45 VA buildings, assessing structural and nonstructural components against the VA Seismic Design Handbook H-18-8 and ASCE 41 through Tier 1 and Tier 2 assessments, with retrofit recommendations engineered building by building. A separate nationwide program grouped 11 geographically dispersed campuses into three regional teams, among them Manchester, Augusta, Asheville and Albuquerque.

At that volume the discipline matters more than the engineering. Every building gets the same method so the results can actually be compared and prioritized.

5 megawatts

At the Houston VA Medical Center, Alares provided construction management and commissioning for a 5.0 MW solar photovoltaic carport system across the campus parking lots, roughly a $21M build carried from groundbreaking through closeout with a manager on site daily.

Parking lots the VA already owned now generate power and shade the cars underneath. That is usually the best renewable move available on a federal campus, because the land is already on the books.

2,200 feet

Earlier studies suggested the Moana Hot Springs resource might extend under the Reno VA Medical Center, but nobody had proof. We designed and installed a test well to a depth of 2,200 feet, ran static and dynamic thermal production testing, and cleared a full environmental assessment under NEPA to a Finding of No Significant Impact, coordinating permits across four agencies.

The testing confirmed direct-use geothermal heating is viable for the campus. Before an owner commits capital to something that large, somebody has to put a well in the ground and find out.

The number that is not in the list

Every project above happened in a building that stayed open. The hospitals kept admitting patients, the plants kept making steam, the courthouse kept sitting. That constraint shapes more of our work than any figure here, and it is the one thing a statistic cannot show.
